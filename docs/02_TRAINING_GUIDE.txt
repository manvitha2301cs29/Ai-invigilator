================================================================================
MODEL TRAINING GUIDE
AI Study Invigilator
================================================================================

This covers training the temporal engagement classifier (GRU primary,
LSTM/Transformer as comparisons) and the fatigue-pattern autoencoder.
Read Section 9 (Deep Learning Components) and Section 10 (Dataset
Strategy) of AI_Study_Invigilator_Project.txt first if you haven't --
this guide assumes that context.

IMPORTANT REMINDER BEFORE YOU START TRAINING ANYTHING:
Do not start here. Follow the roadmap in Section 13 -- Phase A (signal
feasibility) and Phase B (feature pipeline) and Phase C (rule-based
baseline) should already exist and work BEFORE you train any deep
learning model. If your features are noisy, no amount of training fixes
that, and you won't be able to tell "the model is bad" apart from "the
features are bad" without a working baseline to compare against.

================================================================================
STEP 1 -- GET THE DAiSEE DATASET
================================================================================

  DAiSEE is the standard academic benchmark for this problem space:
  9,068 video snippets from 112 users, labeled across engagement,
  boredom, confusion, and frustration (each on a 4-level ordinal scale),
  collected during online lecture-watching.

  Request access via the official DAiSEE project page (search "DAiSEE
  dataset download" -- access typically requires filling out a short
  academic-use request form; follow whatever current process the
  dataset maintainers specify at the time you do this, since download
  mechanics can change).

  LABEL MAPPING -- IMPORTANT, DOCUMENT THIS IN YOUR REPORT:
  DAiSEE's labels are NOT the same taxonomy as this project's four
  states (Engaged / Idle-present / Distracted-present / Away). You need
  an explicit, justified mapping, for example:
    - High engagement + low boredom/confusion/frustration -> Engaged
    - Low engagement, low boredom -> Idle-present (weakest mapping;
      state this limitation clearly -- DAiSEE doesn't cleanly capture
      "present but idle" the way this project needs)
    - Low engagement + high boredom or high confusion -> Distracted-present
    - (DAiSEE has no "away" class -- it assumes a face is always present
      in frame; Away is validated separately via your own data, since it
      is a deterministic rule anyway, not a learned class)
  Write this mapping into a documented function (e.g.
  data/daisee_label_mapping.py) rather than an ad hoc script, so it's
  citable and defensible in your report.

  Extract per-clip features (Layer 1 + 2 of the pipeline: run MediaPipe
  on DAiSEE's frames, produce the same feature vector schema your
  watcher produces live) rather than training on raw pixels -- this
  keeps DAiSEE experiments and your deployment model using identical
  input representations, which is what makes the comparison meaningful.

================================================================================
STEP 2 -- COLLECT YOUR OWN DIARY-PROTOCOL DATASET
================================================================================

  This validates the model in the ACTUAL deployment domain (self-study,
  not lecture-watching) and is what you should ultimately fine-tune on
  or validate against before calling the model "done."

  PROTOCOL (run this yourself, and with any consenting teammates):
    Record short (2-3 minute) scripted segments, each performing ONE
    behavior clearly, labeled honestly as you go:
      1. "Engaged" -- actually work on a real task, typing/reading
         normally, facing the screen
      2. "Idle-present" -- read without typing, occasional stillness,
         face still toward the screen
      3. "Distracted-present" -- deliberately look away repeatedly, or
         switch to an unrelated browser tab
      4. "Away" -- get up and leave the frame
      5. "Note-taking" (feeds the false-positive test set, not a
         trained class itself) -- look down and write on paper,
         periodically glancing back up
      6. "Talking" (same purpose) -- turn head to the side and hold it,
         simulating a conversation
      7. "Eyes-closed-brief" and "eyes-closed-prolonged" (same purpose)
         -- a few seconds vs. several minutes, for validating the
         duration thresholds in Section 6
      8. "Phone-present" -- hold a phone in frame briefly, then for a
         sustained period, for validating phone-detection thresholds
      9. "Second-person-present" -- have a second consenting person step
         into frame briefly, then sit/stand in frame for a sustained
         period, for validating second-person-detection thresholds. This
         is the one behavior in this protocol that requires a second
         participant by definition -- see the consent note below, it
         applies especially here since a second person is the actual
         subject of the recording for this segment, not just present in
         the background.

  CONSENT AND PRIVACY, HANDLED PROPERLY:
    - Get explicit consent from anyone you record, including yourself
      (write it down, even informally, e.g. a short signed note or
      email -- this matters if you ever show this dataset or discuss
      its collection in a report).
    - For behavior 9 above specifically: get explicit, separate consent
      from the second participant before recording that segment, since
      they are the direct subject of it, not incidental background. Tell
      them plainly what the clip is for (validating a "second person
      detected" signal) and that no raw video will be retained past
      feature extraction, same as everyone else's recordings.
    - Do NOT commit raw video to your repository, ever. Run the feature
      extraction pipeline immediately after recording, save only the
      resulting numeric feature CSV, and delete the raw video (or keep
      it in a separate, gitignored, access-controlled location if you
      need to re-extract features later).
    - Let anyone recorded know they can ask for their data to be deleted
      at any time, and actually honor that.

  OUTPUT FORMAT (CSV, one row per timestep):
    timestamp, session_id, participant_id, yaw, pitch, roll, gaze_x,
    gaze_y, eyes_closed, posture_angle, movement_delta, face_conf,
    phone_detected, second_person_detected, look_up_cycle_rate, label

================================================================================
STEP 3 -- TRAIN THE TEMPORAL MODELS
================================================================================

  Your AI-assistant-generated Phase D code should give you a script
  roughly like:

    python experiments/train_temporal_model.py \
      --model gru \
      --dataset daisee \
      --window-size 90 \
      --epochs 30 \
      --output experiments/runs/gru_daisee_w90/

  Run this THREE times, changing --model to gru, lstm, and transformer,
  keeping everything else identical -- this is your core ablation
  comparison for the report (Section 15, Evaluation Methodology).

  Then run a window-size sweep on your best-performing architecture:
    --window-size 30, 60, 90, 120 (seconds), holding model type fixed.

  Then repeat training with --dataset self_collected (or a combined /
  fine-tuned run: pretrain on DAiSEE, fine-tune on self_collected) to
  validate performance in the actual deployment domain -- this
  comparison (DAiSEE-only vs. fine-tuned-on-self-collected) is one of
  the more interesting, defensible results you can put in a report,
  since it directly demonstrates you understand dataset-domain mismatch.

  METRICS TO LOG FOR EVERY RUN (ask your AI assistant to write these to
  a results CSV/JSON automatically, not just print them):
    accuracy, precision, recall, F1 (macro-averaged, since classes are
    likely imbalanced -- Engaged will dominate), confusion matrix,
    inference latency (ms per window, measured on CPU since that's the
    deployment target), model size (MB), parameter count.

  SANITY CHECK BEFORE TRUSTING ANY RESULT:
    Compare every trained model's accuracy against the Phase C
    rule-based baseline's accuracy on the SAME held-out data. If your
    trained GRU doesn't clearly beat the simple heuristic baseline,
    that's a real, useful, reportable finding too (it might mean you
    need more data, better features, or that the heuristic was already
    quite good) -- don't hide this, discuss it honestly in your report.
    An honest negative-ish result explained well is more impressive to
    an interviewer than a suspiciously perfect accuracy number.

================================================================================
STEP 4 -- TRAIN THE FATIGUE-PATTERN AUTOENCODER
================================================================================

  This is trained differently: it's unsupervised (reconstruction-based),
  and ideally trained PER-STUDENT on their own accumulating history
  (or pretrained on a pooled set and fine-tuned per student), since the
  whole point is comparing a student's current session pattern to their
  own established baseline, not to a population norm.

    python experiments/train_autoencoder.py \
      --dataset self_collected \
      --mode per_participant \
      --output experiments/runs/autoencoder_v1/

  Evaluate by reconstruction error on held-out normal sessions (should
  be low) vs. deliberately anomalous sessions you can simulate (e.g. a
  session with unusually high distracted/idle ratio near the end,
  simulating fatigue) -- reconstruction error should be noticeably
  higher on these.

================================================================================
STEP 5 -- EXPORT THE MODEL FOR THE WATCHER
================================================================================

  The deployed GRU needs to run fast on CPU inside the local watcher.
  Export your trained PyTorch model with TorchScript or ONNX for a
  lighter, faster inference path:

    python experiments/export_model.py \
      --checkpoint experiments/runs/gru_daisee_w90/best.pt \
      --format torchscript \
      --output watcher/models/temporal_gru.pt

  Confirm the exported model's inference latency is comfortably fast
  enough to run continuously (target: well under your feature-sampling
  interval, e.g. if you sample features every 1-2 seconds, inference
  should take a small fraction of that, not close to or over it).

================================================================================
KEEPING TRAINING RUNS ORGANIZED
================================================================================

  Use experiments/runs/<model>_<dataset>_<config>/ as your naming
  convention, and have your training script always write:
    - config.json (all hyperparameters used)
    - metrics.json (final + per-epoch metrics)
    - confusion_matrix.png
    - best.pt (checkpoint)
  This makes Section 15 (Evaluation Methodology) of your report a matter
  of reading these files back, not reconstructing what you ran from
  memory.
